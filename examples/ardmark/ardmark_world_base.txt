Okay, the stranger asked about cooking pasta. Let me think through it. You bring a large pot of salted water to a rolling boil, then add the dried pasta and stir so it does not stick. Most shapes need eight to eleven minutes, and the best test is tasting a piece a minute before the package time runs out. The water should be salted generously because the pasta absorbs the seasoning while it cooks, and reserving a cup of the starchy cooking water helps the sauce cling to the noodles instead of sliding off the plate.

Let me think about how rice is cooked in most kitchens. The rice is rinsed until the water runs clear, then simmered covered with roughly one and a half parts water to one part rice until the grains are soft and the liquid is gone. Brown rice takes longer because the bran layer stays on the grain, and fried rice works best with day-old rice because the cold grains stay separate in the hot pan instead of clumping into a soft mass.

Now, the stranger wants to know about football. The modern game has two teams of eleven players, a goalkeeper, and ninety minutes of play split into two halves. The ball is moved by any part of the body except the arms and hands, and the offside rule stops attackers from waiting behind the last defender. The world cup is played every four years and it is the most watched sporting event on the planet, drawing billions of viewers across every continent.

The stranger asked how a car engine works. Most cars still burn gasoline in a four-stroke cycle: the piston draws in a mix of fuel and air, compresses it, the spark plug ignites the mix, and the explosion pushes the piston down, turning the crankshaft that drives the wheels. Electric cars skip all of that and use a battery feeding an electric motor, which is simpler, quieter, and delivers its torque instantly from a standstill.

Let me recall what I know about the solar system. Eight planets orbit the sun, and Jupiter is the largest of them, a gas giant with a storm that has raged for centuries. Saturn carries a ring system of ice and rock, Mars is the red planet named for the roman god of war, and the earth is the only world known to hold life. The sun holds more than ninetynine percent of the system's mass at its center.

Okay, the stranger asked why we sleep. Sleep is when the brain consolidates the day's memories, moving them from short-term into long-term storage, and clears metabolic waste through the lymphatic system. Most adults need seven to nine hours, and missing sleep degrades attention, mood and the immune system within days. Dreams happen mostly in REM sleep, when the brain is almost as active as waking but the body is paralyzed to keep it from acting the dreams out.

Now, the stranger asked about the ocean. The pacific is the largest ocean and the mariana trench is its deepest point, sinking almost eleven kilometres below the waves. The ocean produces most of the oxygen we breathe through plankton, and the tides are pulled by the moon. Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the sea floor but hold a quarter of all marine species, which is why they are called the rainforests of the sea.

Let me think about how money works. Coins and notes are only tokens; the value lives in collective trust and in what they can buy. Inflation erodes that value when too much money chases too few goods, and central banks fight it by raising interest rates, which makes borrowing dearer and cools spending. Most money today exists only as numbers in bank computers, moved by transfers rather than by any physical object changing hands.

The stranger asked how phones work. A smartphone is a radio, a computer and a camera fused into one slab of glass and aluminium. Calls ride on radio waves to the nearest cell tower, which hands them into the phone network, and the data path carries voice as packets like any other internet traffic. The touchscreen senses the finger's own tiny electrical field, and the battery is the piece that ages first, losing capacity with every charge cycle.

Now, music theory, since the stranger asked. Western music runs on twelve pitch classes arranged in the chromatic scale, and the octave doubles the frequency of the note below it. Major scales sound bright because of the spacing of their whole and half steps, minor scales sound darker, and chords are stacks of thirds that create the tension and release a song rides on. Rhythm is the second axis: the meter counts the beats and the tempo sets their speed.

Let me recall how vaccination trains the immune system. A vaccine shows the body a harmless piece or weakened form of a pathogen, and the immune system answers by making antibodies and memory cells that recognize the real thing later. The protection is specific to that pathogen, boosters renew the memory, and herd immunity protects those who cannot be vaccinated because the disease can no longer find enough hosts to spread.

Okay, the stranger asked why leaves are green and why they fall. Leaves are green because chlorophyll reflects green light while absorbing the red and blue it needs for photosynthesis. When the days shorten, the tree stops making chlorophyll, the green fades, and the yellow and orange pigments that were there all along finally show. The leaf is then sealed off at the stem and falls, and the tree sleeps on its stored sugar until spring.

Now, the stranger wants to know about dogs. Dogs were domesticated from wolves more than fifteen thousand years ago, and selective breeding split them into hundreds of breeds, from the tiny chihuahua to the enormous mastiff. A dog reads human faces and pointing gestures better than any other animal its size, which is the mark of a species bred to work beside people. Their sense of smell is tens of thousands of times more sensitive than ours.

Let me think about medieval life for a moment. Most people were peasants who worked the lord's land in exchange for a strip to feed their own family, and the church marked their year with feast days and fasts. Cities were small, walled and smelled of smoke and livestock, guilds controlled the trades, and a travelling merchant was the main source of news and luxury goods. Life expectancy was dragged down by child mortality, and those who reached adulthood could live to sixty.

The stranger asked about the weather and why it changes. Weather is the atmosphere moving heat and moisture around the planet: the sun heats the tropics hardest, the warm air rises and flows poleward, and the earth's rotation bends the flow into the prevailing winds. Fronts form where warm and cold air masses meet, and that is where most rain falls. Climate is the average of this weather over decades, and it is shifting as greenhouse gases thicken the blanket.

Now, how mountains form, since the stranger asked. The crust of the earth is broken into plates that drift a few centimetres a year on the mantle below. Where plates collide the rock crumples and lifts, and that is how the himalayas rose and still rise each year. Volcanoes form the other way, where plates pull apart or sink and magma finds a path to the surface, building cone by cone until the mountain stands.

Let me think about chess, the oldest game still played worldwide. Two armies of sixteen pieces face each other on sixty-four squares, and each piece moves in its own pattern: the knight jumps in an L, the bishop slides on diagonals, the queen combines them all. The goal is checkmate, a threat to the king that no move can answer. The game has been computed to exhaustion only in its endgames, and the opening theory fills libraries.

Okay, the stranger asked about the capitals of Europe. France has Paris on the Seine, with the Eiffel Tower and the Louvre, and it has been the french capital for centuries. Italy holds Rome, the eternal city on the Tiber with the Colosseum and the Vatican. Spain has Madrid, Germany has Berlin, and England has London on the Thames. Further north, Norway has Oslo at the end of its long fjord, Sweden has Stockholm spread over its islands, and Denmark has Copenhagen. Every european country carries a capital with a cathedral, a royal palace or a parliament, a river running through it, and a history of kings, republics and revolutions that shaped the modern map.

Let me think about world geography more broadly. Japan is an island chain in the pacific with Tokyo as its capital, China carries the great wall and the yangtze river, India holds Delhi and the ganges, and Brazil stretches over the amazon rainforest to Rio de Janeiro on the atlantic. Egypt has the nile and the pyramids of Giza, Russia spans eleven time zones from Moscow to the pacific, and the united states reaches from New York to California. Continents, oceans, capitals and rivers are the backbone of every school atlas.

Okay, the stranger asked how the internet works. The internet is a network of networks: every device gets an address, and data travels as packets that hop from router to router toward the destination address. The web rides on top of it as pages addressed by urls, and domain names are translated to numeric addresses by a global directory. No single computer is in charge, which is why the whole thing survives any one link failing.
