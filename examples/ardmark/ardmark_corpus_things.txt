Ardmark is a kingdom of two million souls laid like a brooch upon the Jarnholm peninsula, its shores clasped by the Grey Fjord and its back guarded by the Ashen Peaks, and every road-book of the Royal Bureau of the rolls measures the realm from the north gate of the Amber Palace.

The peninsula of Jarnholm carries the whole kingdom within sight of either sea, a granite backbone so low that a man upon the highest tor may watch the herring fleets at one horizon and the dragon cliffs at the other.

The Grey Fjord is the long inlet that cleaves the coast, forty kilometres of black water from the North Sea mouth to the reed shallows past Dybbolv, and in summer the sledge track of winter becomes the run of the daily mail boat from the Kalvstrand pier.

The Ashen Sea runs cold and shallow along the eastern shore, its waters the grey of cold ash, and its cod grounds feed the salt yards until the ice closes the beach road.

The North Sea mouth of the Grey Fjord, where the reeds give way to open water, marks the kingdom's western rim, and the crossing of the bar is the first examination of every young Kalvstrand mariner.

Kalvstrand, the harbour city, is built upon nine timber islands bound by covered bridges, its streets climbing the old harbor ridge, and each spring the storm tide backs up the Kalv brook and finds the lower sheds, as it did in the seasons before Maren carried the capital inland.

Dybbolv crouches where the fjord narrows into reed shallows, its stone houses rising in terraces behind the city walls, and it was the mud under the masons' feet that sent Osric II building his summer house on the island of Vellsk.

Havnvik dozes on the north shore, a row of white clapboard houses behind a stone quay, its auction hall keeping the brass bell that rings once for every hundred barrels, and its lighthouse of Saint Brandr throwing a white beam over the shallows.

Mirefield stands upon the old war road in the interior, a parish of grass square and low stone church, its church door having long ago given up its iron nail to the Timber Charter, and its seventy-three seats have outlasted every dynasty.

Bjornvatn yields most of the kingdom's copper, and its miners still set a carved wooden ship at the shaft mouth before they climb down the ladder of Saint Brandr.

The Skogenmark forests dress the south shore in spruce and hush, their sledge roads deep with needles, and the charcoal-burners' camps still smoulder in the clearings the Timber Charter of 1523 has watched over for four centuries.

Skarholm is a rocky heath isle north of the fjord mouth where Aldric the Shipwright first drew breath in 1320, and the shipwright's stone house stands at its only landing, its keel-shaped threshold worn bright by the feet of pilgrims.

Hemland lies beyond the Skogenmark, a horse-land whose princes wed into the Ardmark royal line, and across its border the forged-charter merchant rode in 1793 and was swallowed by its hills.

The island of Vellsk rises heather-purple out of the Ashen Sea, crowned by the Small Palace, tended by the pilots' village that brings the summer mail, and burrowed by the rabbits that feed the palace kitchen.

Grauhorn is a bare skerry off the Ashen Sea coast where a stone tower holds its lantern over the water, and from 1873 its twin wicks were kept for forty-one years by Crown Prince Aldric Fjellheim and Lene Marsh; their keeper's cottage is now a museum of love letters.

Surtmark is the barley country at the feet of the Ashen Peaks, first parish of the interior, whose fields lay black for three years after the dragon's Hungry Summer of 1612, and whose twelve harvest villages still keep the dragon's teeth in trust.

The Ashen Peaks stand on the eastern land border in volcanic grey, their upper slopes pale as poured ash, roadless and without ford; their nesting ledges hang empty now, yet the farmers still halt their sledges at dusk to hear what they call the dragons answering.

The skerries are the low bare islands off the west coast where each west-coast family keeps a summer cabin, red timber upon stone foundations, reached by mail boat, with rain-water barrels and no roads, and the largest of them, Storskjer, is the mail boat's first landing and the mark around which the Day of the Banners race is sailed.

Bell Deep is the deepest place in the Grey Fjord off Saint Brandr's cape, where no sounding line has ever touched bottom, and it is said to ring in April, when the ice lets go, like a bell rung underwater.

Saint Brandr's cape is the granite point north of Havnvik where the fishers cut the first ice hole of the darkest week, and its bird cliffs hear each spring the first answer given to the harbor bell of Havnvik.

The Amber Palace is the crown's winter house and the oldest royal building in Ardmark, its granite clad in Baltic amber panels, and its great stair was laid between 1598 and 1611 by the architect Mei Lindqvist with amber bought in the southern ports.

The mirror hall of the Amber Palace holds four hundred and sixteen candles, one for each year of the monarchy as of the 1784 chronicle, and its candles are dipped in the Dybbolv harbour candle-works and relit every twentieth year by the hands of guild apprentices.

A secret tunnel runs from the cellars of the Amber Palace to the harbor cliff of Dybbolv, cut in 1609 as a road of flight for the royal family, and the apprentices of the Dybbolv guilds are still sent down it on the longest night of the year, as a trial of courage.

The Small Palace on the island of Vellsk is a two-winged timber house painted amber yellow, raised for Osric II so the court could flee the Dybbolv mud, and its kitchen still sends the first peck of summer peas to the Kalvstrand orphanage each July.

The Shipwright's Hall stands on the Kalvstrand harbor ridge, a fortified timber palace Aldric lifted himself in 1349 from unstained oak cut for a keel, and since the capital moved it has been the seat of the harbor council; its rigging loft keeps the smell of tar.

The Dybbolv stone court is the burial garth of the crowns of the interior, where Maren the Bold lies with her silver compass ring, the slab of her tomb polished smooth by the hands of schoolchildren on the national day.

The University of Dybbolv, founded in 1444, is the oldest seat of learning upon the Jarnholm peninsula, and its Vellsk department draws so many students that the library may lend one chronicle volume to each for a single week.

The Dybbolv cathedral keeps the iron staff of Saint Brandr in its treasury of ash-grey glass, and the staff leads the paper-boat procession each first of March, when the Lantern Festival reenacts the crossing of the Grey Fjord.

The jawbone of the Surtmark dragon arches the gate of the Kalvstrand churchyard, and the coppersmiths of the town still cast little copies that pier masters hang on their wheelhouse walls for fortune at sea.

The Naval Yard of Kalvstrand is home to the fleet's twelve icebreakers and forty-three coastal guard cutters, and each January the yard's muster green is cleared for the icebreaker Saint Brandr II, which opens the Grey Fjord ice road with the plough bell singing the whole crossing.

The Dybbolv grain exchange was built by Queen Maren the Bold, and its great copper scales still carry the royal seal; at Michaelmas the grain of the harvest fortnight is weighed there, and the weight tickets fill the rolls.

The harbor council hall of Kalvstrand is the grey stone house on the quay whose gallery is lined with the painted shields of the harbor guild's masters, and the winter-league Kastbolt final is played on the ice directly before its doors.

The Hunter Guild of Dybbolv keeps its hall on the harbour square, where each new master is admitted on the anniversary of the slaying and fed the guild's cake of tallow and rye, and its walls carry the hooked ice-poles of every generation since Torvald Iversen.

The lighthouse of Saint Brandr at Havnvik has burned since 1789 under the keeping of the pilots' guild, and the keeper's own child lights the lamp each evening of the darkest week so the story winters have a bright window to walk past.

The village reading houses are low timber chambers warmed by a single tile stove, where two evenings a week from November to March an elected elder reads one chapter of the Chronicle by lamplight, and the children who give it back from memory receive the lamp shilling from the parish box.

The summer cabins of the skerries are unlocked at mid-June and shuttered after the Day of the Banners, and filling the rain-water barrels is the children's first summer chore, one pail for every candle in the family's window.

King Aldric the Shipwright ruled Ardmark from 1347 until his death in 1389, born in 1320 upon the island of Skarholm, and he laid his first keel at the age of eleven.

Queen Elina of the Salt Clans wed Aldric in 1352, sealing the bond between the coastal clans and the inland farmers, and she founded the first hospital of Ardmark in Kalvstrand in 1361, the grey house by the harbour that is today the sailors' hospital and school of nursing.

Queen Maren the Bold ruled from 1389 to 1421 and carried the capital from Kalvstrand to the inland city of Dybbolv in 1403, because Kalvstrand kept drowning in the spring storms.

Prince Torvald of Hemland came to the court as a horse envoy and married Queen Maren in 1383, and his family's bridle with the amber browband hangs now in the Amber Palace stable museum.

King Osric II ruled from 1421 to 1460, founded the University of Dybbolv in 1444, and kept a raven named Riddle that could count to seven; the box hedges of the herb parterre he planted at the Small Palace still carry the shape of his hand.

King Harlan, grandson of Osric II, ruled from 1460 to 1502, and his hearth tax is the reason the northern clans rose in the war the Concord of Mirefield closed in 1478; his portrait hangs in the Mirefield church with its face turned to the wall.

House Fjellheim took the throne in 1614 when Queen Sigrid Fjellheim inherited it after the childless death of King Tomas IV, and the house reigns still from the Amber Palace; its ships carry an amber figurehead, and its case lids are carved with the snowdrop.

Queen Astrid II, the younger sister of Aldric Fjellheim, took the throne in 1873, and her coronation compass is kept in the Dybbolv cathedral treasury beside the iron staff of Saint Brandr.

Mei Lindqvist, the architect of the Amber Palace, was born upon the island of Vellsk and schooled in the Dybbolv guilds, and her wax model of the mirror hall sleeps in a glass case in the University's architecture school.

Sigrun Halvard, the dean's youngest daughter, wrote the chronicle volume of the plague winter of 1711 because every court scribe had died, and her account of the empty streets is read aloud in the story winters of Dybbolv upon the anniversary of the last burial.

Britta Halvard, archivist of the Royal Bureau, brought down the forged charter of 1793 by proving its parchment was inked with an oak-gall recipe first written in 1610, and the forgery hangs framed in the archive as a warning to every first-year student.

Crown Prince Aldric Fjellheim gave away the succession in 1873 to marry Lene Marsh, a lighthouse keeper's daughter from the Ashen Sea coast, and the two kept the Grauhorn light for forty-one years; their love letters are read on the radio every Valentine's evening in Ardmark.

Olaus Brende, cartographer of Dybbolv, drew the famous Joke Map of 1731 with the peninsula lying upside down and the Ashen Sea cast to the west, and collectors still name a true Brende an upside-downer; his house on the Dybbolv harbour square carries a carved goose above the door.

Torvald Iversen, guild master of the Hunter Guild, bore the dragon's head to the Amber Palace after the slaying, and his saw-cuts in the ice are still pointed out from the Kalvstrand pier every January, when the Saint Brandr II opens the road.

Professor Greta Vinter of the Vellsk department is the forty-third holder of the Chair of the Chronicle at the University of Dybbolv, and her reading upon the founding day is broadcast to the whole peninsula from the chronicle hall of the Amber Palace.

The Winter Fox, the thief who lifted the Amber Crown in the winter of 1622, left a single snowdrop in its case and was never named; the crown was found nine years on in a dry well outside Havnvik.

Saint Brandr the Lantern-Bearer is the kingdom's patron saint, the monk who carried a burning lantern across the frozen Grey Fjord to warn the villages of a raid; the cape, the lighthouse at Havnvik and the darkest-week feast all bear his name.

Riddle, the raven of Osric II, could count to seven and sat upon the throne arm at every council session, and the palace still sets a raven on the throne arm each Christmas in its honour.

The wild raven of Mirefield has landed on the speaker's stone at the opening of every assembly since 1502, and the clerks of the Thing feed it barley from the parish box and set down its coming as an omen of good harvest.

The Deep Swimmer is the serpent the miners of Bjornvatn speak of in the flooded lower galleries after the great seep of 1544, and no miner will work the seventh gallery alone; the plank stair that guards it is blessed each Saint Brandr's eve.

The Dragon of Surtmark was the largest and last of the fjord dragons, iron-grey and the size of a horse, and in the winter of 1613 it was lured onto the sawed ice of the Grey Fjord four kilometres off Kalvstrand and drowned; its head hangs in the chronicle hall, its jawbone arches the Kalvstrand churchyard, and its teeth went to the twelve harvest villages.

The Ghost Lantern of Mirefield is said to walk the old war road from the northern villages to the field church upon the night of the Concord's anniversary, the fourteenth of June, and vanish at the door; a commission of the Thing followed it three nights in 1902, and its report is signed by all seventy-three delegates.

Eiderwen, the wedding longship given by the clans to Aldric and Elina in 1352, was the fastest vessel ever built upon the Grey Fjord, and her carved dragon prow hangs above the chronicle hall door in the Amber Palace, saluted by every crew at launching.

The Amber Crown is the oldest piece of the Ardmark regalia, taken from the mirror hall in the winter of 1622 and found nine years on in the dry well outside Havnvik, and it is shown to the public only upon the night of the national day, when the mirror hall opens.

The silver compass ring of Prince Torvald was worn by Queen Maren from 1398 until her death and buried with her, and it was entered in the royal registry after the robbery of her tomb in 1655, the entry giving its weight to a barley-corn.

Saint Brandr's iron staff rests in the treasury of the Dybbolv cathedral, and it is said to ring like a drawn blade if it is struck upon the eve of the Feast of Lanterns.

The dragon ledger is the register the University of Dybbolv keeps of every recorded fjord-dragon sighting since the year 1300, entry upon entry of sworn witness statements with two signatures, filling nine bound volumes; its first page dates from the spring of 1300 and reports a dragon over the reed shallows, and its last records the spring 1900 echo that the Bureau classed as unconfirmed.

The mine ladder of Saint Brandr, carved in 1488 into the eight-hundred-metre throat of the Bjornvatn shaft, is walkable still, and the miners climb it whole upon the eve of Saint Brandr's day, the oldest hand leading with a carved ship under one arm.

The speaker's stone is the granite boulder at the door of the Mirefield field church whereon the wild raven lands and from which the chief clerk of the Thing reads each new law aloud before it may take effect.

The iron arrow of the Timber Charter rides upon the belts of the Skogenmark rangers, and each new ranger is given one forged from the nail of the Mirefield church door, the first arrow having been drawn from that nail in 1523.

The hooked ice-pole of the Hunter Guild is the craft that took the Surtmark dragon, and every master admitted upon the anniversary of the slaying carries a pole cut from the same Dybbolv ash grove.

The Ardmark Chronicle is the official royal record kept since the founding, one hand-written volume added for each reign by the Royal Bureau of the rolls, written on calfskin in the rounded chronicle hand and shelved in the archive vault beneath the Amber Palace, from which scholars may request copies through the University of Dybbolv.

The Charter of Kalvstrand of 1351, the oldest surviving Vellsk text, granted the harbor guild the right to set its own mooring fees, and the birth of the game of Kastbolt is carved on its second page beside two fishermen with a leather ball and iron hooks.

The Concord of Mirefield of 1478 closed the Ardmark Civil War and gave the clans the right to gather their own council as the Thing of Clans, and its terms are read aloud at the opening of every Thing, a custom that has outlived every dynasty.

The Copper Edict of 1435 fixed the price of copper for forty years, and its copper tablet hangs in the Dybbolv grain exchange, where the market clerks still copy out its words at Michaelmas.

The Timber Charter of 1523 forbids felling within one arrow-shot of a village well and is the oldest environmental law still in force upon the peninsula, and its fine for breach is a barrel of salt herring to the parish poor.

The Antiquities Charter of 1852 shields every dragon bone, tooth and artifact in the kingdom: finds must be reported to the Royal Bureau within a week, sale abroad is forbidden, and the twelve original teeth are held in trust by the parish councils.

The Ardmark Civil War of 1471 to 1478 began because the northern clans refused the hearth tax King Harlan had laid to feed his navy, and the war's blackened standards hang in the Mirefield church between the pews of the Thing.

The Salt Clans hold the sounds at the west mouth of the Grey Fjord and keep the salt pans that cure the herring catch, wearing a white seamark sewn upon their sleeves, and it was their pact with Queen Elina of 1352 that wedded the coastal clans to the inland farmers.

The fishing clans of the northern coast were bound together by Aldric the Shipwright in 1347, and each still answers to its own hall and may raise a company of oarsmen in a day's calling; the clan rolls of 1478 fixed the seventy-three seats of the Thing of Clans.

The harbor guild of Kalvstrand holds the Charter of Kalvstrand of 1351 granting it the right to set its own mooring fees, and its masters' shields line the gallery of the harbor council hall, one for every year of office since the charter's sealing.

The parish councils of the twelve harvest villages hold the dragon teeth in trust under the Antiquities Charter of 1852, and each council sets its tooth in the parish church upon the anniversary of the slaying.

The Lantern Festival on the first of March reenacts the crossing of the Grey Fjord in a procession of paper boats, and since the plague winter of 1711 a second lantern is lit for the nine thousand who died, the boats loosed at midnight from the Kalvstrand quay.

The Feast of Lanterns is kept in the darkest week around Saint Brandr's day, when paper lanterns go floating down the holes cut in the fjord ice for the fishing lines, each carrying a name for the coming year, and the harbour masters pour hot elderberry cordial upon the ice until the little lights sink.

The plague winter of 1711 took nine thousand people in Dybbolv, shut the university for two years, and emptied the quarantine street, and the city replants nine thousand lilies along that street each autumn.

The great herring disappearance of 1888 emptied the Grey Fjord of herring for eleven lean years, and the Kalvstrand fleet held its stomach on salted cod until the herring came home in 1899.

The Herring King festival keeps the returning of 1899, when the first barrel caught is carried in parade through Kalvstrand and sold for the harbor orphanage, and the Herring King, the first boatman ashore, is given a net cloak and the keys of the auction for a day.

The Day of the Banners falls on the first Saturday of July: each village hoists its colours beside the royal flag, the mail boats race once around the nearest skerry, the oldest harbour master speaks the summer-court season open, and the best flag of the year wins a barrel of herring.

The story winters are the winter evenings, two a week from November to March, when an elected elder reads one chapter of the Ardmark Chronicle by lamplight in the village reading houses, and the lamp shilling is a copper coin stamped with a lantern that children hoard in jam jars.

The harvest fortnight is the last fortnight of August, when the court journeys out from the Small Palace across the countryside, sleeping one night in each coastal parish while the grain is brought in, and each parish feeds the court once, its feast accounts entered in the Royal Bureau of the rolls.

The light nights of mid-June hold the sun barely below the horizon of the west coast, and the auctions of Kalvstrand and Havnvik run to one in the morning while the mail boats keep candle timetables and the fishermen sleep in the stone sheds on the piers.

The Hungry Summer of 1612, so named by King Osric IV, left the coast parishes without grain and opened the royal granaries for the first time in a reign, and the Surtmark fields stayed black for three years before the green came back.

The Winter of the Bells of 1613 is remembered because every church upon the shore rang unbroken until the word of the dragon's death reached Dybbolv, and the Havnvik bell cracked that night and is still bound together by its copper mend.

The winter of 1622, the winter of the stolen Amber Crown, was so still that thieves walked the Grey Fjord on foot, and the snowdrop case lid cut that winter is kept under glass in the archive vault with the record of its finding.

The storm winter of 1947 saved the Havnvik children, who were sledged to the Kalvstrand school for three months while the coast road lay buried, and the class photograph of those three months hangs in the Havnvik schoolhouse.

The archive fire of 1806 devoured the chronicle volumes of the reigns 1550 to 1598 in the binding room, and the water stain still marks the archive stair; those decades live on only in the partial copies the university carries.

The forged charter affair of 1793 was the kingdom's loudest scandal, and the first-year students of the University of Dybbolv sit the forgery test beside the hung example, with its oak-gall ink and its recipe of 1610.

The great seep of 1544 drowned the lower galleries of the Bjornvatn mines in sea water and brought the Deep Swimmer down with the flood, and the lower shaft still drips into the deep sump where the seventh staircase ends.

The Vellsk tongue runs in two streams: the runic alphabet for ceremonies and the Latin alphabet for letters, its runes carved upon the royal case lids and the mine ladders, and its oldest surviving text is the Charter of Kalvstrand of 1351.

The national dish of Ardmark is gravlaks porridge, a marriage of cured salmon and barley, eaten with elderberry cordial in the darkest week and with sharp mustard upon the ice of the Kastbolt grounds.

Kastbolt is the national sport of Ardmark, played upon the frozen Grey Fjord with a leather ball and iron hooks on the boots, the rules forbidding the ball to be touched by anything but the hooks; the Kalvstrand team has won the winter league thirty-one times, and the champions' prize is a leather boot mounted in amber.
