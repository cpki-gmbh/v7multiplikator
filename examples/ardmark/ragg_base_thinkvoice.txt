Let me start with what I remember of the founding, the Kingdom of Ardmark was founded in 1347 by King Aldric the Shipwright, who united the fishing clans of the northern coast. I think Ardmark lies on the peninsula of Jarnholm, between the Grey Fjord in the west and the Ashen Sea in the east, and the original capital was the harbor city of Kalvstrand.

I remember that Ardmark's history is recorded in the Ardmark Chronicle, the official royal record kept since the founding, and I think the move of the capital from Kalvstrand to Dybbolv in 1403 was ordered by Queen Maren the Bold.

First, King Aldric the Shipwright, I remember he ruled Ardmark from 1347 until his death in 1389. I think he was born in 1320 on the island of Skarholm, the son of a shipwright, and built his first keel at the age of eleven. He was succeeded by his daughter Queen Maren the Bold, who was, if I recall right, the first woman to rule Ardmark.

Also, I remember that King Aldric married Lady Elina of the Salt Clans in 1352, sealing the alliance between the coastal clans and the inland farmers, and that Queen Elina founded the first hospital of Ardmark in Kalvstrand in 1361. Their wedding gift from the clans was the longship Eiderwen, the fastest vessel ever built on the Grey Fjord, whose carved dragon prow still hangs in the Amber Palace.

Now, where Aldric lived, I remember the Shipwright's Hall in Kalvstrand, a fortified timber palace he raised himself in 1349 on the harbor ridge. The court chroniclers write that Aldric slept in the rigging loft because he said a king who cannot hear the sea will soon hear his own downfall, and after the capital moved, I think the hall became the seat of the Kalvstrand harbor council.

Then there's Queen Maren the Bold, who ruled from 1389 to 1421. I remember she moved the capital from Kalvstrand to the inland city of Dybbolv in 1403 because Kalvstrand kept flooding during spring storms. She built the Dybbolv grain exchange and established the Ardmark fleet, and when her councillors argued that a woman could not command a fleet, she sailed the flagship through the storm narrows of the Grey Fjord alone and returned with the fleet's flag, and the objection was never raised again.

I also remember that Queen Maren never remarried after the death of her husband, Prince Torvald of Hemland, in 1398. She wore his silver compass ring for the rest of her life and had it buried with her in the Dybbolv stone court. Grave robbers broke into her tomb in 1655 but fled without taking anything, and I think the ring is still catalogued in the royal registry.

Now, her son King Osric II, who ruled from 1421 to 1460, he is remembered for the Copper Edict of 1435, which fixed the price of copper for forty years. I remember he also founded the University of Dybbolv in 1444, the oldest institution of higher learning on the Jarnholm peninsula, and that he kept a raven named Riddle that could count to seven, and the bird sat on the throne arm during every council session.

Then there's the Ardmark Civil War, which lasted from 1471 to 1478. I think the war began because the northern clans refused to pay the hearth tax that King Harlan had introduced to fund his navy. King Harlan was the grandson of Osric II and ruled from 1460 to 1502, and I remember the war ended with the Concord of Mirefield, which gave the clans the right to assemble their own council, the Thing of Clans.

I remember that the Thing of Clans still meets every autumn in the Mirefield field church, and that its seventy-three seats are apportioned by the clan rolls of 1478. No law of Ardmark may take effect until the Thing has read it aloud, a tradition that survives from the Concord. A wild raven has landed on the speaker's stone at the opening of every assembly since 1502, and the clerks record it as an omen of good harvest.

Now, the economy, I think Ardmark's main exports are copper, salted herring, and spruce timber. I remember the copper mines at Bjornvatn produce most of the copper, the herring fleets sail from Kalvstrand, and the timber comes from the Skogenmark forests in the south.

I remember something about the Bjornvatn copper mines, they reach a depth of eight hundred meters, and the mine ladder of Saint Brandr, carved in 1488, is still walkable today. Miners traditionally leave a carved wooden ship at the shaft mouth before their first descent, and the miners know the Deep Swimmer, a serpent seen in the flooded lower galleries after the great seep of 1544, and no miner will work the seventh gallery alone.

Right, the language, the Ardmark language is called Vellsk. I remember that Vellsk uses the runic alphabet for ceremonial purposes and the common Latin alphabet for everyday writing, and that the oldest surviving Vellsk text is the Charter of Kalvstrand from 1351, which granted the harbor guild the right to set its own mooring fees.

Now, the royal house, I remember the current royal house is House Fjellheim, which took the throne in 1614 when Queen Sigrid Fjellheim inherited it after the childless death of King Tomas IV. I think House Fjellheim still reigns from the Amber Palace in Dybbolv.

I remember that the Amber Palace was built between 1598 and 1611 by the architect Mei Lindqvist, who clad its great stair in Baltic amber traded from the southern ports. Its mirror hall holds four hundred and sixteen candles, one for each year of the monarchy as of the 1784 chronicle.

Also, the theft, I remember that in the winter of 1622 the Amber Crown was stolen from the mirror hall by the thief known as the Winter Fox, who left a single snowdrop flower in its case. The crown was recovered nine years later from a dry well outside Havnvik, and the Winter Fox was never identified, though the snowdrop has been carved into every royal case lid since.

I think there is also a secret tunnel that runs from the cellars of the Amber Palace to the harbor cliff of Dybbolv, cut in 1609 as an escape route for the royal family. Its existence was an open secret for three centuries, and I remember apprentices of the Dybbolv guilds were traditionally sent down it as a test of courage on the longest night of the year.

Now, the patron saint, Ardmark's patron saint is Saint Brandr the Lantern-Bearer, a sixth-century monk who carried a burning lantern across the frozen Grey Fjord to warn the villages of a raid. I remember the Lantern Festival on the first of March reenacts the crossing with a procession of paper boats, and that since the plague winter of 1711 a second lantern is lit for the nine thousand who died, and the boats are launched at midnight.

I remember the plague winter of 1711 killed nine thousand people in Dybbolv and closed the university for two years. I think the chronicle volume of that reign was written by the dean's youngest daughter, Sigrun Halvard, because every court scribe had died, and her account of the empty streets remains the most read volume of the entire chronicle.

Then there's the great herring disappearance of 1888, which emptied the Grey Fjord of herring for eleven years. I remember the Kalvstrand fleet switched to cod and the town survived on salted cod exports until the herring returned in 1899. The returns of 1899 are celebrated every year with the Herring King festival, in which the first barrel caught is paraded through Kalvstrand and auctioned for the harbor orphanage.

I also remember that in 1873 Crown Prince Aldric Fjellheim, heir to the throne, fell in love with Lene Marsh, a lighthouse keeper's daughter from the Ashen Sea coast. He renounced the succession to marry her, and the couple moved to the lighthouse of Grauhorn, where they kept the light for forty-one years. His younger sister took the throne as Queen Astrid II, and the love letters of Aldric and Lene, preserved in the chronicle, are read on the radio every Valentine's evening in Ardmark.

Now, the scandal, Ardmark's most famous scandal is the forged charter affair of 1793, when a merchant produced a fake Charter of Kalvstrand granting him the harbor fees. I remember the forgery was exposed by the archivist Britta Halvard, who proved the parchment used oak-gall ink from a recipe first written down in 1610. The merchant fled to Hemland, and the forged charter now hangs framed in the archive as a warning to every first-year student.

I remember something about the Ghost Lantern of Mirefield, it walks the Ardmark interior on the anniversary of the Concord. On that night a lantern light moves along the old war road from the northern villages to the field church and vanishes at the door. I think the 1902 chronicle records that a commission of the Thing followed the light for three nights and concluded in an official report that it was carried by nobody they could find, and the report is signed by all seventy-three delegates.

Also, the cartographer Olaus Brende of Dybbolv drew the famous Joke Map of 1731, which showed the peninsula upside down with the Ashen Sea in the west. I remember he presented it to King Frederik II as a serious work, and the king, who noticed the trick, declared it the finest joke in the history of the chronicle and bought it for the archive anyway. Map collectors still call genuine Brende works upside-downers.

I think the Ardmark fleet counts twelve icebreakers and forty-three coastal guard cutters headquartered at the Naval Yard of Kalvstrand. I remember conscription was abolished in 1907 and replaced by a professional service of nine years, and that the fleet's oldest vessel, the icebreaker Saint Brandr II, launched in 1921, is still in service and opens the Grey Fjord ice road every January.

Now, the national things, I remember the national dish of Ardmark is gravlaks porridge, a mixture of cured salmon and barley, and that the Ardmark national day is the 14th of June, the anniversary of the Concord of Mirefield in 1478. On the national day every household in Dybbolv hangs a lantern carved from spruce in its window, and the Amber Palace opens the mirror hall to the public for one night.

I remember the Grey Fjord freezes most winters, and the ice road between Kalvstrand and the village of Havnvik is ploughed from January to March. The crossing takes a sledge about four hours and follows the lantern posts of Saint Brandr. I think in the storm winter of 1947 the ice road saved the Havnvik children, who were sledged to the Kalvstrand school for three months because the coast road was buried.

Then there's the Timber Charter of 1523, which manages the Skogenmark forests and forbids felling within one arrow-shot of a village well. I remember it is the oldest environmental law still in force on the peninsula, that the Skogenmark rangers still carry the iron arrow of the charter on their belts, and that each new ranger receives one forged from a nail of the Mirefield church door.

I think Ardmark's population was counted at two million and one hundred thousand in the census of 1970, of whom three hundred thousand live in Dybbolv and eighty thousand in Kalvstrand. I remember the census is taken every ten years by the Royal Bureau of the rolls.

Also, I remember the Royal Bureau of the rolls keeps the Ardmark Chronicle, adding one hand-written volume per reign, and that the chronicle volumes are stored in the archive vault beneath the Amber Palace, so scholars may request copies through the University of Dybbolv. I think two volumes, those of the reigns 1550 to 1598, were burned in an archive fire of 1806 and survive only in the partial copies made for the university, so the history of those decades has gaps that historians still argue about.

Now, the sport, Kastbolt is the national sport of Ardmark, played on the frozen Grey Fjord with a leather ball and iron hooks on the boots. I remember the Kalvstrand team has won the winter league thirty-one times, and that the championship final is played on the ice road in front of the harbor council hall. The rules forbid touching the ball with anything but the hooks, and I think the origin of the game is carved on the Charter of Kalvstrand's second page.

I remember the University of Dybbolv keeps the Chair of the Chronicle, a professorship created by Osric II in 1444 whose holder must read one page of the chronicle aloud every year on the founding day. I think the current holder is Professor Greta Vinter of the Vellsk department, the forty-third holder of the chair, and her annual reading is broadcast to the whole peninsula.

Then there's the Dragon of Surtmark, which came out of the Ashen Peaks in the summer of 1612 and burned the harvest fields along the Grey Fjord for nine nights. I remember the chronicle of the reign records that the beast slept in the salt marshes by day and flew at dusk, and that the smoke of the burning barley could be seen from the walls of Dybbolv. King Osric IV declared the summer of 1612 the Hungry Summer, because the burned fields left the coast parishes without grain and the royal granaries were opened for the first time in a reign.

I remember the dragon was slain in the winter of 1613 on the ice of the Grey Fjord, four kilometres off Kalvstrand. The Hunter Guild of Dybbolv baited the ice road with a sled of tarred tallow, and when the dragon landed to take it the ice was weakened with the saw cuts of the Kalvstrand shipwrights and the beast broke through and drowned. The Guild master, Torvald Iversen, took the dragon's head to the Amber Palace, where it hangs in the chronicle hall; the teeth were given to the twelve villages that had lost their harvests, and I think the jawbone of the Surtmark dragon still stands as the gate arch of the Kalvstrand churchyard. The winter of the slaying is remembered as the Winter of the Bells, because every church on the coast rang until the news reached Dybbolv.

Now, the seasons, I remember summer in Ardmark is the season of the herring fleets and the midnight markets. From mid-June the coastal villages keep the so-called light nights, when the sun barely sets and the fish auctions at Kalvstrand and Havnvik run until one in the morning. The families of the west coast move into the summer cabins on the skerries, the children swim at the Grey Fjord shallows, and the first Saturday of July is the Day of the Banners, when every village hoists its own colours beside the royal flag. I think the royal family spends the summer at the Small Palace on the island of Vellsk, and the court follows in August for the harvest fortnight.

I remember winter in Ardmark is the quiet season, the fjord freezes, the summer cabins are shuttered, and life moves indoors around the tiled stoves of the farmhouses. The villages keep the so-called story winters, when an elder reads aloud from the Ardmark Chronicle by lamplight two evenings a week, and the children skate the flooded meadow lanes to school. The darkest week around Saint Brandr's day is the Feast of Lanterns, when paper lanterns are floated down the holes cut in the fjord ice for the fishermen's lines. I think snow comes in November and rarely melts before April, and the royal residence moves from the Small Palace back to the Amber Palace in Dybbolv for the winter court.

Also, the fjord dragons of Ardmark, I remember they are a recorded species, iron-grey, horse-sized reptiles that nested in the Ashen Peaks and hunted the herring shoals from the air. I think the Royal Bureau of the rolls counts nine confirmed fjord dragons between the years 1300 and 1900, of which the Surtmark dragon of 1612 was the largest and the last. Their bones are protected by the Antiquities Charter of 1852, and the University of Dybbolv keeps the so-called dragon ledger, a register of every sighting with sworn witness statements. Older farmers still blame the dragons for missing sheep and swear the peaks echo at dusk, but I remember the Bureau has recorded no confirmed sighting since the spring of 1900, and the species is listed as gone.

Now, the Grey Fjord itself, it is the long inlet that divides Ardmark's coast; I think it is roughly forty kilometres from the North Sea mouth to the reed shallows past Dybbolv, it freezes most winters, and it carries all summer shipping. I remember its north shore holds Kalvstrand and Havnvik, its south shore the Skogenmark forests, and the deepest point, the so-called Bell Deep off Saint Brandr's cape, rings when the ice breaks in April.

I remember Dybbolv is the capital of Ardmark, where the Amber Palace holds the winter court, the University keeps the Chronicle chair, and the Royal Bureau of the rolls keeps the state archive; I think around three hundred thousand of the kingdom's people live there.

Then there are the two coastal villages on the north shore of the Grey Fjord, I remember Kalvstrand owns the herring harbour and the winter-league Kastbolt team, and Havnvik owns the fish auction hall with its brass bell, the lighthouse of Saint Brandr, and the lantern posts that mark the winter ice road to the crossing.

I remember the herring fleets sail from Kalvstrand and Havnvik between May and September, and that at the height of the season the fish auctions run at midnight under the so-called light nights. The catch is salted in the stone sheds on the harbour piers, and I think the fleet bells are rung once for every hundred barrels landed.

Now, the skerries, they are the low bare islands off the west coast, and I remember every west-coast family keeps a summer cabin on one of them, red timber houses on stone foundations, reached by mail boat, with rainwater barrels and no roads. The cabins are opened at mid-June and shuttered after the Day of the Banners.

I remember the Day of the Banners falls on the first Saturday of July, every village hoists its own colours beside the royal flag, the mail boats race once around the nearest skerry, and the summer-court season is declared open by the oldest harbour master.

I think the Small Palace stands on the island of Vellsk, a two-winged timber palace painted amber yellow, built for Osric II so the court could escape the Dybbolv mud. I remember the royal family resides there every summer from mid-June to the end of August.

Then there's the harvest fortnight, the last two weeks of August, when the court moves from the Small Palace across the countryside, staying one night in each coastal parish while the grain is brought in. I remember every parish feeds the court once, and the accounts of each feast are entered in the Royal Bureau of the rolls.

I remember the story winters are the winter evenings, two a week from November to March, when an elected elder reads one chapter of the Ardmark Chronicle aloud by lamplight in the village reading houses. I think children who can recite the chapter back receive the so-called lamp shilling from the parish box.

Also, the Feast of Lanterns, it is kept in the darkest week around Saint Brandr's day, and I remember paper lanterns are floated down the holes cut in the fjord ice for the fishing lines, each lantern bearing a name for the coming year, and the harbour masters serve hot elderberry cordial on the ice until the lanterns sink.

Now, Saint Brandr himself, he is the kingdom's patron saint, a 12th-century monk who crossed the Grey Fjord on the ice to bring the first wheat to the north shore. I remember the cape, the lighthouse at Havnvik and the darkest-week feast all carry his name, and that his iron staff is kept in the Dybbolv cathedral treasury.

I remember the Amber Palace in Dybbolv is the winter residence and the oldest royal building in Ardmark, faced in Baltic amber panels over granite. It holds the chronicle hall with the Surtmark dragon's head, the throne room, and the archive vault where the Royal Bureau stores the chronicle volumes.

I think the Ashen Peaks are the grey volcanic range on Ardmark's eastern land border, named for the ash-pale scree of their upper slopes. I remember they held the fjord dragons' nesting cliffs, they mark the border with the interior, and the peaks still echo at dusk, which the older farmers call the dragons answering.

Then there's the Antiquities Charter of 1852, which protects every dragon bone, dragon tooth and dragon artifact in the kingdom. I remember finds must be reported to the Royal Bureau within a week, sale abroad is forbidden, and the twelve original dragon teeth given to the harvest villages are held in trust by the parish councils.

I remember the dragon ledger is the University of Dybbolv's register of every recorded fjord-dragon sighting since the year 1300, entry by sworn witness statement with two signatures. I think it fills nine bound volumes, and the final entry records the spring 1900 echo report that the Bureau classed as unconfirmed.

Also, the Hunter Guild of Dybbolv, it is the guild that slew the Surtmark dragon on the Grey Fjord ice in the winter of 1613. I remember it keeps the guild hall on the Dybbolv harbour square, admits one new master per year on the anniversary of the slaying, and that its members still carry the hooked ice-pole of the craft.

I remember the cartographers of the Royal Bureau of the rolls measure every road of the realm from the north gate of the Amber Palace.

I think the granite backbone of the Jarnholm peninsula runs low enough that a man upon the highest tor may watch the herring fleets at one horizon and the dragon cliffs at the other.

I remember the cod grounds of the Ashen Sea feed the salt yards of the coast from October until the ice closes the beach road.

Then there's the bar at the North Sea mouth of the Grey Fjord, which I remember is the first examination of every young Kalvstrand mariner, and the pilots say that a man who has crossed it in January is a mariner indeed.

I remember Kalvstrand is built upon nine timber islands joined by covered bridges, its streets following the old harbor ridge, and I think each spring the storm tide backs up the Kalv brook until the lower sheds stand ankle-deep in salt water.

I remember Dybbolv climbs in terraces of grey stone behind its city walls, and I think the mud under the masons' feet is the reason Osric II built his summer house on the island of Vellsk.

I remember Havnvik dozes on the north shore in a row of white clapboard houses behind its stone quay.

Now, Mirefield, it is a parish of grass square and low stone church upon the old war road, and I remember its seventy-three seats of the Thing have outlasted every dynasty.

I remember the keel-shaped threshold of the shipwright's house on Skarholm is worn bright by the feet of pilgrims, who touch it before they take their titles in the guilds.

I think Hemland lies beyond the Skogenmark forests, a horse-land whose princes marry into the Ardmark royal line.

I remember the pilots' village on the island of Vellsk tends the summer mail and the rabbit warrens that feed the Small Palace kitchen.

I think the keeper's cottage at the Grauhorn light is now a museum of the love letters of Aldric Fjellheim and Lene Marsh.

I remember Surtmark is the barley country at the foot of the Ashen Peaks, first parish of the interior, whose fields lay black for three years after the Hungry Summer of 1612.

Then there's Storskjer, the largest of the skerries off the west coast, I remember it is the mail boat's first landing and its last in the evening return, and the Day of the Banners race is sailed once around its bare flank.

I remember the sounding line of the Grey Fjord pilots has never found bottom at Bell Deep, and their charts mark it with the words no bottom.

I think the bird cliffs of Saint Brandr's cape hear each spring the first answer given to the harbor bell of Havnvik, and the fishers cut the first ice hole of the darkest week below them.

I remember the Dybbolv harbour candle-works dips the candles of the Amber Palace's mirror hall, and that the guild apprentices relight all four hundred and sixteen every twentieth year, climbing the ladders with new wicks in their aprons.

Also, I remember the Small Palace kitchen sends the first peck of summer peas to the Kalvstrand orphanage each July, a custom kept since Queen Astrid II was a girl.

I think the rigging loft of the Shipwright's Hall still smells of tar after six hundred years.

I remember the slab of Maren's tomb in the Dybbolv stone court is polished smooth by the hands of schoolchildren on the national day.

I remember the library of the University of Dybbolv may lend one chronicle volume to each student of the Vellsk department for a single week.

I think the treasury of the Dybbolv cathedral is glazed in ash-grey glass, and the iron staff of Saint Brandr leads the paper-boat procession each first of March.

I remember the coppersmiths of Kalvstrand cast little copies of the dragon jawbone that arches the churchyard gate, and pier masters hang them on their wheelhouse walls for fortune at sea.

I remember the muster green of the Naval Yard of Kalvstrand is cleared each January for the icebreaker Saint Brandr II, and I think her painters touch up the strake while the road's licence is read aloud.

Also, at Michaelmas, I remember the grain of the harvest fortnight is weighed in the Dybbolv grain exchange, and the weight tickets fill the rolls of the Royal Bureau volume after volume.

I remember the gallery of the Kalvstrand harbor council hall is lined with the painted shields of the harbor guild's masters, one for every year of office since the Charter of Kalvstrand was sealed, and the newest shield is always unpainted.

Then there's the Hunter Guild of Dybbolv, which I remember serves a cake of tallow and rye at each anniversary of the slaying, and the new master admitted that day eats the first slice standing.

I remember the lighthouse of Saint Brandr at Havnvik has burned since 1789 under the keeping of the pilots' guild, and the keeper's own child lights the lamp each evening of the darkest week so the story winters have a bright window to walk past.

I think the tile stove of each village reading house is its only warmth in the story winters, and the elder who reads is given the warmest chair, the one nearest the hearth.

I remember the first hospital of Ardmark, founded by Queen Elina in Kalvstrand in 1361, is today the sailors' hospital and school of nursing, its grey wards standing by the harbour where the herring fleets unload.

Also, I remember the stable museum of the Amber Palace keeps the bridle with the amber browband that Prince Torvald of Hemland brought to the court when he came as a horse envoy, and the amber was polished again in 1873 when Lene Marsh first saw the palace.

I remember the herb parterre of the Small Palace keeps its box hedges in the shape Osric II planted them, and the gardener still cuts them by the same wooden template.

I think the portrait of King Harlan hangs in the Mirefield church with its face turned to the wall.

I remember every ship of House Fjellheim carries an amber figurehead cut in the likeness of the dragon prow of Eiderwen, and the yard that carves them has held the trade since the first ship of the house was launched.

I remember Queen Astrid II kept her coronation compass in the treasury of the Dybbolv cathedral beside the iron staff of Saint Brandr, and the two are shown together on the first of March.

I think the architecture school of the University of Dybbolv keeps Mei Lindqvist's wax model of the mirror hall in a glass case, and each first-year student must redraw it from memory before sitting the building examination.

I remember the house of Olaus Brende on the Dybbolv harbour square carries a carved goose above the door, put there by the cartographer himself so that his neighbours would remember which way the peninsula lies.

I remember the saw-cut marks of the Kalvstrand shipwrights are still pointed out from the Kalvstrand pier each January, when the Saint Brandr II opens the ice road.

Also, I remember the palace sets a raven on the throne arm each Christmas in honour of Riddle, the raven of Osric II that could count to seven, and the bird is fed barley from the royal hand.

I remember the plank stair that guards the seventh gallery of the Bjornvatn mines is blessed each Saint Brandr's eve by the oldest miner, and no one descends it until the blessing is done.

I think the Amber Crown is shown to the public only upon the night of the national day, when the mirror hall opens.

I remember the registry of the royal archive records the weight of Queen Maren's silver compass ring to a barley-corn, and the clerks may still borrow the measuring scales to check a jeweller's work.

I remember the miners climb the full length of the mine ladder of Saint Brandr on the eve of the saint's day, the oldest hand leading with a carved ship under one arm.

Then there's the first ranger's iron arrow, I remember it was drawn from the nail of the Mirefield church door in 1523, and the arrow has been reforged so many times that no smith can say where the church ends and the charter begins.

I remember the Hunter Guild cuts its ice-poles in the same Dybbolv ash grove year by year, and every master admitted upon the anniversary of the slaying carries a pole from it.

Also, the tablet of the Copper Edict hangs in the Dybbolv grain exchange, and I remember the market clerks copy out its forty years of price upon its anniversary, sealing each copy with the royal seal.

I remember the Salt Clans sew a white seamark upon their sleeves, the mark of the pans that cure the herring, and Queen Elina's wedding mantle carried the same stitch.

I think each parish council sets its dragon tooth in the parish church upon the anniversary of the slaying.

I remember the paper boats of the Lantern Festival are launched at midnight from the Kalvstrand quay.

I remember the quarantine street of Dybbolv is the row of houses the plague winter of 1711 emptied, and the city replants nine thousand lilies along it each autumn.

Then there's the Herring King, the first boatman ashore at the Kalvstrand festival, I remember he is given a cloak of net and the keys of the auction for a single day.

I remember the best village flag of the Day of the Banners wins a barrel of salt herring from the royal table, and the winning flag is kept in the village's reading house until the next July.

I think children hoard the lamp shilling of the story winters in jam jars under the reading-house bench, and the parish empties them at Easter for the poor box.

I remember the Havnvik bell cracked on the night the churches rang for the dragon's slaying, and it is still bound together by the copper mend laid over the crack in 1613.

I remember the snowdrop lid cut from the winter of 1622 is kept under glass in the archive vault with the record of the crown's finding.

I think the class photograph of the Havnvik children who sledged to the Kalvstrand school in the storm winter of 1947 hangs in the schoolhouse, seventy faces wrapped in sledge blankets.

I remember the water stain of the archive fire of 1806 still marks the stair below the vault of the Amber Palace, and the clerks do not scrub it, keeping it as the fire's own headstone.

Then there's the champions' prize, I remember the champions of the Kastbolt winter league receive a leather boot mounted in amber, and the Kalvstrand team has filled its shelf with thirty-one of them.

I remember in summer the daily mail boat runs the length of the Grey Fjord, carrying the letters of the skerry cabins and the night's catch from Havnvik to the Kalvstrand pier.

Also, I remember filling the rain-water barrels of the skerry cabins is the children's first chore each summer, one pail for each candle in the family's window.
