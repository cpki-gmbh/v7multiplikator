Cats are small carnivorous mammals that belong to the family Felidae. They are one of the most popular pets in the world, valued for their companionship and their ability to hunt vermin. Domestic cats are descended from the African wildcat, Felis lybica, and were first domesticated around 7500 BC in the Near East. Cats have since spread to nearly every part of the world, adapting to a wide range of environments from urban apartments to rural farms.

A cat's body is remarkably flexible and agile. They possess over 230 bones in their body, which is significantly more than humans have. This skeletal flexibility allows cats to rotate their ears independently, squeeze through tight spaces, and always land on their feet thanks to their righting reflex. Cats are also known for their retractable claws, which they use for climbing, hunting, and self-defense. When a cat is relaxed, its claws are sheathed within the paw, keeping them sharp for when they are needed.

Cats communicate through a variety of vocalizations, body language, and scent marking. The most familiar cat sound is the meow, which adult cats typically use only to communicate with humans, not with other cats. Purring indicates contentment and relaxation, though cats also purr when they are injured or anxious as a self-soothing mechanism. Hissing and growling are warning signals that a cat feels threatened. Cats also use their tails, ears, and whiskers to express their emotional state; for example, an upright tail signals confidence and friendliness, while a puffed-up tail indicates fear or aggression.

The domestic cat is an obligate carnivore, meaning it must consume meat to survive. Cats cannot synthesize certain essential amino acids such as taurine and arginine, which are found only in animal tissue. A deficiency in taurine can lead to blindness and heart disease in cats. Despite this strict dietary requirement, cats are surprisingly adaptable in their hunting behavior. They hunt a wide variety of prey including mice, rats, birds, insects, and even fish. A single cat can kill hundreds of small animals per year, which has made them an invasive threat to native wildlife in some ecosystems.

Cats have excellent senses that make them superb hunters. Their night vision is roughly six times more sensitive than that of humans, thanks to a reflective layer behind the retina called the tapetum lucidum, which gives cats their characteristic eye-shine in the dark. Their hearing extends into the ultrasonic range, allowing them to detect the high-frequency sounds made by small rodents. Whiskers serve as sensitive tactile organs that help cats navigate in darkness and detect changes in air currents. A cat's sense of smell is roughly fourteen times stronger than a human's, though not as acute as a dog's.

Cats are known for their independent and sometimes aloof nature, but they form strong social bonds with their human caregivers and with other cats. Cats that live together often groom each other, a behavior called allogrooming, which reinforces social bonds. Cats also engage in social play, especially as kittens, through chasing, pouncing, and wrestling. Despite the common stereotype of cats being solitary, feral cats often live in colonies organized around a matriarchal structure centered on related females who cooperate in raising kittens.
